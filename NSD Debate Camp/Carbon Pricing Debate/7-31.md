# Permissibility and Presumption
"ought to" in the resolution implies a moral obligation to do something. The AFF is trying to prove some proactive requirement, and the job of the NEG is to disprove the AFF. 
The AFF has to prove that we are obligated to use a carbon tax. In debate, there will be a little spat about who should win. It's agreed upon that AFF should go for obligation and NEG should go for prohibition. The NEG should win the round if they prove that it's permissible. 

If you can show that AFF was at a more disadvantaged position, then if it's a tie, then AFF should win.

In a constructive speech, have 1-3 arguments why presumption and permissibility go to your side. 

## Triggers
The point of ethics is to tell you what's right or wrong by telling you what or what not to do.
### Skeptical Conclusion
If we have humanity on one side of a cliff and the true philosophy is on the other side, think of the gap in between as the skeptical conclusion. Whenever we justify ethical theory, we're trying to construct a strong bridge across the gap. If the skeptical conclusion is true, then it is contesting both frameworks. With moral skepticism, permissibility is true. 
When you write your framework for a debate case, you can put a built-in trigger. When you're explaining your framework:
- Justify the skeptical conclusion
- Provide a solution to the skeptical conclusion, which is your framework.
## Contractarianism
The contractarian framework starts its syllogism by first discussing the question of Internalism. Internalism says that ethical theories can't create obligations unless internally motivating. This is in contrast to externally motivating force.

This is a good skep trigger in debate. If your opponent disproves contractarianism but doesn't prove that their framework is internally motivating, then you can extend externalism to the skep trigger. Without obligations, the skep trigger happens which triggers permissibility. 

The external world skep says that we can't know whether we are all in the matrix.
