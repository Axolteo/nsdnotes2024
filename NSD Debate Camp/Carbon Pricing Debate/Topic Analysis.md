## What is Carbon Pricing?
Carbon pricing is an environmental strategy aimed at reducing greenhouse gas emissions by assigning a monetary cost to carbon emissions.

The most common types of carbon pricing are emissions trading systems (ETS) and carbon taxes. The former sets and overall emission limit and allocates permits for trading, whereas the latter imposes a fee on emissions to increase their cost and incentivize reductions.

### 1. Carbon Pricing
**Definition:** A method for reducing global warming emissions by putting a price on carbon emissions.
### 1. Carbon Tax
**Definition:** A tax imposed on the carbon content of fossil fuels to incentivize reducing carbon emissions
### 3. Cap-and-Trade System (Emissions Trading System)
**Definition:** A system where a limit (cap) is set

Carbon tax requires emitters to pay a defined amount of tax in accordance with their emissions. Carbon pricing aims to incentivize emitters to reduce GHG emissions through putting a tax on carbon emissions

## Carbon Tax
A carbon tax is like a straight-up fee for polluting. Think of it like when you get a parking ticket. Every time a company releases a ton of carbon dioxide in the the air, they have to pay a set amount of money for each ton. It's predictable: if you pollute this much, you pay this much.

## Cap and Trade
Cap-and-trade is a bit more like a game with trading cards. The government sets a "cap" on the total amount of CO2 that can be emitted, and then it issues permits (like trading cards) for that amount. Companies need these permits to pollute. If they want to emit more CO2, they need to buy more permits from others who have extras.

## Key Differences

|                                   | **Carbon Tax**                                                                         | **Cap-and-Trade**                                                                                               |
| --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Predictability vs Flexibility** | Predictable costs. You know exactly how much you'll pay for every ton of CO2 you emit. | Flexible limits. The total emissions are capped, but the cost of permits can vary based on content.             |
| **Control Aspects**               | Controls the cost of emitting CO2. Companies can plan because they know the price.     | Controls the total amount of emissions. The environment benefits because the cap sets a hard limit.             |
| **Market Dynamics**               | Simple fee structure. Everyone pays the same rate per ton of CO2                       | Creates a market. Companies can buy and sell permits, leading to fluctuating prices based on supply and demand. |

## Important factors
### Carbon Taxes
1. **Tax Rate**
	1. *Initial Rate:* The starting price per ton of carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions.
	2. *Escalation Path:* How the tax rate will increase over time.
2. **Scope and Coverage**
	1. *Sectors Included:* Which sectors of the economy are covered (eg., transportation, industry, electricity).
	2. *Types of Emissions:* Whether the tax applies to all greenhouse gases or just CO2.
3. **Revenue Use**
	1. *Revenue-Neutral:* Whether the revenue is used to reduce other taxes or returned to the public.
	2. *Earmarking*: Specific uses for the revenue, such as funding renewable energy projects, improving public transportation, or assisting low-income households.
4. **Distributional Effects**
	1. *Impact on Different Income Groups*: How the tax affects low-income versus high-income households.
	2. *Geographic Impact:* Differences in impact on urban versus rural areas.
5. **Economic Impacts**
	1. *Competitiveness*: Effects on the competitiveness of domestic industries
	2. *Employment*: Potential job creation or loss in different sectors
6. **Environmental Effectiveness**
	1. *Emission Reductions*: Projected reductions in greenhouse gas emissions
	2. *Complementary Measures:* Additional policies or regulations that support the carbon tax
7. **Political Feasibility**
	1. *Public Support*: Level of public acceptance and support.
	2. *Political Landscape*: Likelihood of passage and implementation given the current political environment.
8. **Compliance and Administration**
	1. *Ease of Implementation*: Complexity and cost of administering the tax.
	2. *Monitoring and Enforcement:* Mechanisms to ensure compliance and prevent evasion.
9. **Exemptions and Thresholds**
	1. *Universal Application:* Few or no exemptions.
	2. *Exemptions*: Certain industries or activities may be exempt, or small emitters below a threshold may be excluded.
10. **Interaction with Other Policies**
	1. *Standalone Tax:* Operates independently
11. **Adjustment Mechanisms
	1. *Static Tax*: Tax rate remains fixed regardless of external factors.
	2. *Dynamic Adjustments*: Rate adjustments based on economic conditions, emissions levels, or technological advancements.

## Cap and Trade Important Factors
1. **Cap Settings**
	1. *Stringency*: The overall limit on emissions and how stringent it is.
	2. *Reduction Pathway*: How the cap will decrease over time to achieve long-term emission reduction goals
2. **Cap Setting and Reduction Pathway**
	1. *Stringent Caps*: Programs with more aggressive caps, such as the EU ETS, aim for significant reductions.
	2. *Gradual Caps*: Programs like California's gradually reduce the cap to balance economic and environmental goals.
3. **Coverage**
	1. *Sectors Included*: Which sectors are covered
4. **Allocation of Allowances
	1. *Free Allocation vs. Auctioning*: Whether allowances are given for free, auctioned, or a combination of both.
	2. *Initial Distribution*: How initial allowances are distributed among entities.
	3. *Revenue Use:* How the revenue from auctioned allowances is used.
