# Three parts to an argument
### Claim
The argument made.
### Implication (occasionally)
An action to perform in response to the claim (vote me, do this, etc.)
### Warrant
The evidence/backing for the claim. The "meat" of every argument. An **empirical** warrant represents information that is known. An **analytical** warrant uses logic and reasoning. "Analytical" means that it's your own words, not something you're citing from someone else. "Empirical" means that the claim is referencing a piece of evidence in a debate. You could quote an author that uses logic and reasoning and call that empirical, but for debate, it would still be analytical.
### Impact
How the claim impacts something positively/negatively. It figures out what this argument means for people. Usually you'd also need to use more warrants and impacts to argue that the impact will occur. 
# Examples
### Claim
Spiders are the greatest animal
### Warrant
They're scary and control pests
### Implication
Keep spiders as pets over any other animal

### Response
Spiders are bad to keep as pets. Their scariness is a bad attribute, not a good one, since they cause fear in many people, and fear makes humans stressed. This stress has been proven to lead to a shorter lifespan and death.[^1] **For a spider to control pests, it would have to roam free in a residence, meaning it would probably get stepped on or cause fear if sighted. Keeping them in a cage would not do anything for pest control either, nullifying the argument that they could control pests.** [^2]

[^1]: This is a good argument and is offensive because it actively goes against the first assumption that scariness is a good trait.
[^2]: this works but it's defensive, unlike saying spiders would increase bug population or there is a better pet to have for pest control.

An alternative pet to have is a cat. Cats are also able t   o keep pest levels down, and they're considered cute and not fear-inducing towards many people (according to polls), meaning cats are better pets to have.
### Claim
Stuffed bears are the best kind of bear
### Warrant
They cannot attack you, unlike other bears.
### Implication
If you wanted to own a bear, you should own a stuffed one.
### Response
The argument that stuffed bears are the best kind of bear because they're safe and do not pose a threat to you is wrong. First, stuffed bears have been proven to have suffocated toddlers and small children, meaning stuffed bears are actually dangerous. Each year, there are twenty-two deaths from teddy bears and one-hundred fifty thousand injuries. **The materials used to create plush toys may also contain heavy metals, such as lead or cadmium, causing long-term health risks and death.**

# Framework
Frameworks describe goals to have when deciding what actions to take. It does not specifically address the resolution at hand, but it can be used when deciding if an action is good or bad. Kind of like a basis of morality. It shows what impacts can happen from the AFF or NEG. 
## Utilitarianism
The most straightforward framework is **utilitarianism**: Do any action that will do good for the most amount of people. Utilitarianism is a form of **consequentialism**. Consequentialism is an attribute that many types of ethics have. 

Regarding carbon pricing, if the AFF debater defends a framework of utilitarianism, then in order for them to win, they need to demonstrate that implementing carbon pricing would increase pleasure and decrease pain, or decrease death, which is a net good. A utilitarian would say that it's fine for people to be poor as long as the whole economy is in good shape. 
## Meta-ethics
**Meta-ethics** are more abstract concepts that describe qualities of ethics. For example, consequentialism is a meta-ethic, and it says that every ethical theory we make should be one that guides our decisions by telling us what outcomes we want to produce.

Other consequentialist ethics are **structural violence** (the outcomes we should avoid are ones of structural violence, like discriminating by race, gender, etc.). 

**Deontology** focuses on the intent behind actions. Even if the outcome of your choice was bad, as long as the intention behind the choice was good, it was a good action.

# 2 Ways to Justify Ethics
## 1. Syllogism
Use multiple logical steps to make a logical solution. A "laundry list" of logical steps. If you lose any one argument in the syllogism, everything after it goes away. You can't weigh your framework with it if at least one of the arguments is lost. Generally, it should be as short as possible. Syllogisms provide strong resources on the framework to use against your opponent's framework.
## 2. Independent Justification
You would have the name of the framework and multiple independent reasons that justify that framework. They won't be tied together (a means b, b means c, c means d), they're all independent of each other for justifying the framework. It will usually start with a requirement for ethics, and the ethical framework must be contextual to each individual person. The A-speck argument says that government obligations must be utilitarian. A downside is that you have to have strong claims about what ethics must include. 

Using syllogisms and independent justification along with each other is the strongest way to make sure that the cons of each method are covered by the other. 

# Utilitarian Framework
Utilitarian frameworks care about the consequences of the actions that are produced. The most common type of utilitarianism argument is hedonic act utilitarianism. Hedonic act utilitarianism cares about maximizing pleasure and minimizing pain as a consequence of our actions. There are two categories of util on this front: **"act util"** is the standard and **"rule util"** isn't as commonly used.

**Act util**: We do a calculation of whether our action will produce more pleasure or pain every time we act.
**Rule util**: We do a calculation of whether our action follows a rule that produces more pleasure or pain every time it is put into action.
# Moral Naturalism Meta Ethic
We gain knowledge of the world, including ethical facts of what is right and wrong, based on observation through our five senses. Introspection builds on moral naturalism because it counts as an observation. Moral naturalism cannot deal with "supernatural" facts; only observable facts are counted. This means that abstract concepts do not exist.

Phenomenal introspection is looking inward and making deductions about our experiences in ourselves or things we observe.
## Phenomenal introspection is true:
1. It can bridge the gap from natural facts to moral truths and necessitates hedonism. When I observe an apple, I can introspect and identify redness as an intrinsic property of an apple. Similarly, when I feel pleasure, I can introspect and identify goodness is an intrinsic property of pleasure.
2. Darwinian dilemma- Moral beliefs shift as we evolve which means either moral facts change which contradicts moral realism or evolution led us to moral truth, which is impossible since evolution doesn’t track morality. Hedonism solves since when we introspect for survival, we use the same part of the brain that identifies moral relevance, which is evolutionarily advantageous.
3. Pleasure and pain are intrinsic value and disvalue – everything else regresses – robust neuroscience.
