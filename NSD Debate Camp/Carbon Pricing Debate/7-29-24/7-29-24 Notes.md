# Sociology of Race
When sociologists study race, we're studying how it is that people think about and interact with race. Race is a social construct. The color of our skin being significantly different enough to classify people as different races is purely a social construct, not the color of our skin itself. Concepts like whiteness and blackness are very specific to the current cultural period we live in. It's unique to the time in which we discuss it. Concepts of whiteness and blackness did not exist in ancient Rome; it was more class-based. 

Conceptions of what race is change and vary as time passes. When we're thinking about race, it's important to remember that these conceptions exist within specific times, places and cultures. 

When the transatlantic slave trade was going on, blackness and whiteness in the US were ironed out. If you think about whiteness in the US, the main comparison is about European whiteness. If you were to go to Europe and refer to people with white skin as white people, it would confuse a lot of people. There are Irish people, Danish people, Italian people, etc. The many places in Africa are very diverse, so calling all of them black is a broad generalization. 

In the US, we say whiteness and blackness because they were created for the explicit purpose of dividing socioeconomic groups. If these concepts were created artificially, how is it then that we still use them? It doesn't make much sense. This is one of the areas that Afro-pess talks about. The other aspect of that is that it's ingrained into our society. It creates an implicit bias in our minds. This goes for all forms of oppression. The argument here is simple: everyone, whether we are explicit about it or not, whether we are aware or not, harbors anti-black view. It is an inescapable reality of the society we're in. When you're exposed to media that is anti-black, you internalize them. It severely impacts how you think of questions of race in America. 

**Examples of Implicit Bias:**
There was a study done of juries in the US. They were asked questions about a case and if they sentenced someone to death. The crime was always the same. When juries were under the impression that the perpetrator of the crime was black, they were much harsher in their sentencing even though all of the facts were the same. There are consistent underlying implicit biases, yet the biases vary across socioeconomic status. Everyone's implicit biases are at the core of *systemic racism.* We're not saying that the systems we live in are racist, but they foster anti-blackness. 

Microaggressions are pretty frequent. 

War on Drugs, terms like "thugs" and "punks", black vs white poverty, racism through proxy, etc. are all examples of implicit biases in today's worlds.

Intersectionality shows how some people suffer from multiple types of discrimination (e.g. gay black people). Picture a spiderweb. It has a lot of different threads that make up the web, and we all make up different parts of the web. Some of us touch one of the threads, while others touch multiple. This doesn't mean the people on the outside of the threads aren't discriminated against, but the conjoining of forms of oppression is multiplicative. The combination of homophobia and racism is a lot more serious than either.
