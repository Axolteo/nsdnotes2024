# What is a Kritik?
A kritik (K) is made up of 4 different parts:
1. **Role of the ballot/judge:**
	1. *Role of the ballot*
		1. Where the ballot should go in the round. Talk about what the round should be focused on. 
	2. *Role of the judge*
		1. How the judge should act in the round, what the judge should do. If the role of the judge is an **educator**, the judge must educate debaters about the topic. As a **policy-maker**, the judge looks to the AFF and NEG worlds to see which ones are better.
	3. You can't have both the role of the ballot and the role of the judge. Role of the ballot/judge shows that the debate should care about. It's the framework of the K.
	4. Some examples are to vote for the debater that provides the best argument against ableism or racism or other types of discrimination.
	5. "The role of the ballot is... [role]" 
	6. If the role of the ballot is to promote cheese, there might not be a good argument for why that's the purpose of the round.
	7. Theory of Power shows how your argument says something about the world.
	8. Pedagogies are like an educational strategy to justify the kritik. 
	9. Understand what the theory of power is saying and what type of offense they have. If they have pre or post-Fiat offense, remember that. If your opponent says a racial slur, that's a pre-Fiat because it's not happening in the world proposed. 
	10. Certain topics probably shouldn't be read by certain groups (e.g. Afro-pes by a non-black person). 
2. **Link:** 
	1. This link is a piece of evidence or merely a statement explaining what the opposing team did. It can actually be as simple as saying, "The aff/neg team said/did X." Kritik links vary from things endorsed in the case or speech or things said or done in round.
	2. Dispo is a condition for kicking a K; for example, having three perms or something.
3. **Impact:**
	1. The impact is the reason why what the opposing team did is a bad thing and how it relates to the role of the ballot. These are the equivalent of nuclear war scenarios and extinction claims so commonly made in regular disadvantages.
4. **Alt:**
	1. The alternative is a way to solve for the harm that the opposing team has made because of their previously stated link and impact. Generally, it is usually something as simple as, "Reject this way of thinking," while other alts may be actual actions like performing a "thought experiment," or promoting a different impact framing, or using non-traditional evidence like song and poetry.  
	2. In many ways, you can think of the link and impact as a disadvantage and the alternative as a counterplan. The link and the impact is a reason why the case or the opposing team is bad, and the alternative is the way to solve that bad thing.

Theory introduces arguments considering things we think should be rules in debate. You're making arguments in favor of some rule existing. When we read a theory shell, it's basically like reading a new case. You're reading a case for your opponent breaking something that should be a rule in debate.
## Interpretation
*Debaters must argue (with words).*
Use a positively worded requirement as a rule, and then you'd have to give reasons for that case.
## Violation
*The opponent was juggling in the AC*
Describe how the rule was broken.

Fairness explains how everyone should have an equal shot to win. If there is a debate between two theory arguments, the debaters will want to argue that one is better. Basically like a framework debate.
## Paradigm Issues
Questions about the impact or implication of the theory shell itself is. 
**Drop the debater vs drop the argument**
	Dropping the debater is offense and dropping the argument is defense, so you want to drop the debater more often than not. 
**RVI vs no RVI**
	A reverse voting issue would say that the interpretation is a bad rule and that the violating debater should win because of that. Basically "turn vs no turn".
**Reasonability vs Competing Interpretations**
	Competing interpretations means that the opponent is expecting you to defend a competing rule and justify why that rule is better compared to theirs. The violating debater must justify a competing interpretation.
	Reasonability means that the person defending against the theory shell should not be expected to defend an entire counter rule, they should just defend that what they did was not enough to deserve punishment. First, read a reasonability Brightline. All you're saying is that "this is the point where actions become unreasonable".
Making a counter rule and defending it well allows you to win the debate. 
## Responding:
When you respond to theory, there are two ways: 
1. respond to standards. Show how the opponent is wrong and how the world of debate would be worse with the rule.
2. respond to the violation. Show how you didn't do it or argue over semantics.
## NIB
*"The best argument known to man"*
**Necessity:**
	An argument that you have to respond to in order to win. Defense is not an example of this. Same with a framework; debaters can't win *just* with their framework, meaning that doesn't have necessity.
**Sufficiency:**
	If an argument is capable of winning the round for you.

NIB stands for:
- Necessary
- Insufficient
- Burden

If your opponent has a NEG case and there's some argument about existential skepticism ( an argument that says the world isn't real, so there's no reason to vote AFF) in the middle and they have responses to your case. If the AFF proved that the world existed, you still couldn't turn it. NIBs are basically, "is it turnable or not?" If it can't be turned, it can't gain sufficiency.

Any theory shell with no RVI is an example of something that's necessary but an insufficient burden. If there's no RVI, then you cannot turn theory and win off of it. If you defeated the shell, you couldn't win as a result of beating the shell. 

NIBs are basically arguments that the AFF *has* to respond to, but they can't win by turning them. You could argue that NIBs are unfair and bad for the debate. If you have a theory shell prepped for NIBs, you could argue against them.
