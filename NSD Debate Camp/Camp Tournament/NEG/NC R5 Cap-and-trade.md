> We question this reasoning. <mark style="background: #8a5cf5; color: white; font-size: 24px; padding: 2px">Carbon pricing faces</mark> five <mark style="background: #8a5cf5; color: white; font-size: 24px; padding: 2px">major issues that limit its use for accelerating deep decarbonization.</mark> First, <mark style="background: #8a5cf5; color: white; font-size: 24px; padding: 2px">carbon pricing frames climate change as a market failure rather than as a fundamental system problem.</mark> Second, it places particular weight on efficiency as opposed to effectiveness. Third, <mark style="background: #8a5cf5; color: white; font-size: 24px; padding: 2px">it tends to stimulate the optimization of existing systems rather than transformation</mark>. Fourth, it suggests a universal instead of context-sensitive policy approach. Fifth, it fails to reflect political realities.
> Given these limitations, we propose an alternative approach that targets fundamental transformations of existing sociotechnical systems, such as energy, mobility, or food (i.e., “sustainability transitions”) ([5](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.2004093117#core-r5)). STP entails a mix of contextually and politically sensitive policies that simultaneously drive low-carbon innovation and the decline of fossil fuels.
> <mark style="background: #8a5cf5; color: white; font-size: 24px; padding: 2px">The </mark>underlying <mark style="background: #8a5cf5; color: white; font-size: 24px; padding: 2px">rationale for carbon pricing is</mark> appealing in <mark style="background: #8a5cf5; color: white; font-size: 24px; padding: 2px">its simplicity</mark>: <mark style="background: #8a5cf5; color: white; font-size: 24px; padding: 2px">GHG emissions are </mark>viewed as <mark style="background: #8a5cf5; color: white; font-size: 24px; padding: 2px">a negative </mark>externality <mark style="background: #8a5cf5; color: white; font-size: 24px; padding: 2px">because the social costs</mark> flowing from climate change impacts <mark style="background: #8a5cf5; color: white; font-size: 24px; padding: 2px">are not reflected in the market price</mark> of carbon-intensive goods and services ([6](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.2004093117#core-r6)). <mark style="background: #8a5cf5; color: white; font-size: 24px; padding: 2px">Climate change is framed as the consequence of a market failure</mark> that can be <mark style="background: #8a5cf5; color: white; font-size: 24px; padding: 2px">corrected by placing a price on carbon</mark> so that actors also pay for the social cost of their carbon-intensive activities and reduce their demand for such goods and services.
> <mark style="background: #8a5cf5; color: white; font-size: 24px; padding: 2px">Framing the climate challenge as a market failure</mark>, however, <mark style="background: #8a5cf5; color: white; font-size: 24px; padding: 2px">fails to </mark>seriously <mark style="background: #8a5cf5; color: white; font-size: 24px; padding: 2px">appreciate its scope </mark>and depth. Indeed, the climate challenge has been referred to as a “grand challenge” ([7](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.2004093117#core-r7)) or “super wicked problem”([8](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.2004093117#core-r8)) that has thus far resisted traditional policy approaches.
> We argue that <mark style="background: #8a5cf5; color: white; font-size: 24px; padding: 2px">climate change can be</mark> more appropriately <mark style="background: #8a5cf5; color: white; font-size: 24px; padding: 2px">understood as a system problem. Core societal functions such as heating or mobility, are met through large</mark> and deeply entrenched <mark style="background: #8a5cf5; color: white; font-size: 24px; padding: 2px">sociotechnical systems made up of interconnected tech, infrastructures, regulations, business models, and lifestyles</mark> ([1](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.2004093117#core-r1)). Over many decades, <mark style="background: #8a5cf5; color: white; font-size: 24px; padding: 2px">these systems have become increasingly locked into the combustion of fossil fuels and the</mark> associated <mark style="background: #8a5cf5; color: white; font-size: 24px; padding: 2px">release of GHG emissions</mark>. Consider, for instance, how the design of cities has developed alongside the diffusion of the gasoline-powered personal automobile; how norms about comfort and attire have become entwined with energy-intensive indoor temperature regulation; and how important political and economic interests have become entrenched with fossil fuel-based resource development or electricity provision.
> Addressing the climate challenge, therefore, involves fundamental changes to existing systems, referred to as “sustainability transitions” ([5](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.2004093117#core-r5)). These transitions entail profound and interdependent adjustments in sociotechnical systems that cannot be reduced to a single driver, such as shifts in relative market prices. In mobility, for example, a low-carbon transition might encompass interacting developments around new vehicle technologies (e.g., autonomous electric cars), infrastructures (e.g., vehicle charging stations and high-speed rail), business models(e.g., mobility as a service and intermodal transport), and regulation (e.g., emission performance standards) but also changes in city planning(e.g., reduced urban sprawl) and lifestyles (e.g., telework and local vacations). The market failure framing fails to appreciate the broad scope of the climate challenge and the sweep of system elements that must undergo change. <mark style="background: #8a5cf5; color: white; font-size: 24px; padding: 2px">And so, the resulting solution orientation is far from sufficient.</mark>
